A game design explainer

Passport

Languages you live in, not languages you study.

Design doc · v0.1 August 2026 Status: nothing built yet, on purpose

1. The problem with how you're learning Spanish

Here is a thing that happens to millions of people every year: they decide to learn Spanish, they download an app, and they get good at the app.

They maintain a 500-day streak. They know that the bear drinks milk (el oso bebe leche) — a sentence they will need exactly never. Their little green owl is proud of them. And then they land in Madrid, a waiter asks them something extremely normal, and their brain produces a soft dial tone.

This isn't because they're bad at languages. It's because they were never learning Spanish. They were learning Spanish-flavored trivia. Tapping word tiles is to speaking a language what watching cooking shows is to cooking — genuinely adjacent, weirdly satisfying, and not the thing.

Meanwhile, there's a method with a roughly 100% success rate: move there. Everyone knows someone who studied French for six years and can't speak it, and someone else who moved to Mexico City with nothing and was functional in six months. The second person didn't have a better app. They had stakes. They needed bread, and the bread was behind a conversation.

Linguists have a boring name for the magic ingredient: comprehensible input — hearing the language, slightly above your level, in situations where you genuinely care what's being said.

1Stephen Krashen's "input hypothesis," usually written as i+1: you acquire language from input that's one notch above your current level i. Not five notches (noise), not zero notches (no growth). More on the staircase later.
Add the need to actually produce the language — out loud, to a person, with something on the line — and you get fluency. Remove the stakes and the person, and you get streaks.

So the design question isn't "how do we make flashcards more fun." It's:

Can we build the town?

2. What if you could just... move there?

Here's the game. You wake up in a small Spanish town called Pueblo. You have a tiny apartment, a map, and a problem: nobody here speaks English.

And Pueblo is only the first campaign.

Not "nobody" as in pre-recorded characters with three dialogue options. Nobody as in — every person in this town is a live LLM agent with a name, a job, a personality, a daily schedule, and a memory of every conversation you've ever had with them.

2This is the architecture from the Stanford "Generative Agents" paper (Park et al., 2023) — the famous "Smallville" demo where 25 LLM agents lived in a town, formed opinions of each other, and once spontaneously organized a Valentine's party. We're borrowing the core loop: persona + schedule + observation + memory. The difference: their agents lived for each other. Ours live for you.
Rosa the baker opens at seven and is cheerful until noon. Tomás the fisherman exaggerates. Doña Carmen watches the street from her balcony and misses nothing.

You walk up to any of them and press one button, and then you talk. With your mouth. They hear you, they answer out loud, and they behave like people — they get confused when you're confusing, delighted when you try, and they remember.

And the game is simply: live there. Get breakfast. Make a friend. Do a favor. Get gossiped about. Slowly stop translating in your head, because the town has stopped feeling like Spanish practice and started feeling like your neighborhood.

The shape of the game If you're looking for the genre: it plays like Pokémon Silver — top-down open world, walk everywhere, talk to everyone, regions gated by your progress — but with the structure of GTA: free roam anywhere that's open, and missions you pick up from people in the world, not from a lesson menu. (With, yes, better graphics than the Game Boy Color — the look we're chasing is Alba: A Wildlife Adventure — stylized low-poly Spanish Mediterranean, a warm little diorama you lean over — with a nod to Tunic for camera discipline and light.)

Campaigns, like Age of Empires

This is why the game is called Passport and not Pueblo. Every campaign is a country, a language, and a town you have to make a life in. You don't beat Spanish and unlock a Spanish 2 skill tree — you finish your year in Pueblo, get the stamp, and land somewhere the alphabet might not even be the same.

CampaignCountryLanguageWhat's different about it
PuebloSpainSpanishThe first campaign. Warm, loud, everyone's in your business.
BorgoItalyItalianClose enough to Spanish that your instincts betray you. Cognate traps.
MachiJapanJapaneseNew writing system, and politeness levels that are a whole second axis of getting it wrong.
ColoniaMexicoLatAm SpanishYou already speak "Spanish." Turns out that was Spain Spanish. Everyone can tell.

The campaign structure is a design constraint as much as a feature: it means nothing in this game is allowed to be Spanish-specific. Not the mission system, not the resident architecture, not the world tooling. A new country should be content — a tileset, a cast, a culture file — not a rewrite. Section 8 is largely about how to make that true cheaply.

3. Meet the town

Pueblo is small enough to know and big enough to grow into. It's carved into districts, and the districts are secretly a curriculum — roughly one CEFR level each

3CEFR: the A1 → C2 scale used across Europe for language ability. A1 is "can order coffee slowly," B2 is "can argue with a bureaucrat." Pueblo's districts map to it loosely, the way Pokémon gyms secretly map to a difficulty curve.
— except nobody ever says the word "curriculum," the same way Pokémon never told you gym badges were a difficulty curve.

DistrictLevelLife thereSample missions
La PlazaA1The café, the bakery, the fountain. Greetings, food, numbers, small talk.Order breakfast. Learn three names. Buy bread without pointing.
El MercadoA2Market stalls, haggling, opinions about produce (strong ones).Shop from a list. Haggle Tomás down. Return a fish that has gone wrong.
El BarrioB1Neighbors, gossip, favors, feuds, the past tense (because gossip requires it).Convince Doña Carmen you didn't break her flowerpot. Mediate a dispute. Get invited to dinner.
El AyuntamientoB2The town hall. Forms, appointments, being sent to a different window.Register your address. File a noise complaint. The final boss: get a permit.

Two things make this open-world rather than a course wearing a costume:

Free roam is real. Anywhere that's unlocked, you can wander. Eavesdrop on the plaza. Skip the bakery for a week. Spend an afternoon following Tomás around for no reason. The town doesn't wait for you to finish Unit 3 — it just runs.

Missions come from people, not menus. Rosa asks you to deliver bread to her sister in El Barrio — that's a mission, and its Spanish objective (directions, introductions, relaying a message) is smuggled inside the errand. You accept it by saying yes, out loud, to Rosa. There is no quest log screen with lesson titles. There's just a town full of people who need things and a growing list of people who owe you one.

Story missions, side missions, and the inversion that matters

Borrowing GTA's structure properly means three tiers, not one:

TierWhat it isExample
The storyYour integration arc. A spine of maybe a dozen missions per campaign that move you from stranger to resident, and gate the districts.Find a place to live. Get the coach to vouch for you. Survive the town hall.
Side missionsOptional errands and favours from anyone who'll talk to you. Unlimited, generated, endlessly repeatable in spirit if not in detail.Deliver the bread. Settle who owes whom. Talk Tomás down on the price of a fish.
Just livingNo mission at all. The plaza at eight in the evening. Chatting because you felt like it.Ask Doña Carmen about her day and get twenty minutes of unsolicited neighbourhood gossip.

Here's the part worth being deliberate about. In GTA, side content is optional flavour — you can beeline the story and finish the game. In Passport that priority inverts. The story is a dozen conversations; fluency needs hundreds. So the side missions aren't garnish, they're the actual mechanism — the place the volume of speaking practice comes from — and the story exists mostly to give that wandering a direction and a reason to care.

Which means the side-mission generator is a core system, not a stretch goal. Residents need to be able to want things: an errand, an opinion, an introduction, a small grudge that needs mediating. A town where everyone has run out of things to ask you is a town you stop visiting.

And it changes what a "quest log" is. A living town shouldn't sprout floating exclamation marks over people's heads — that's the fastest way to turn residents back into vending machines. Instead you find out the way you'd find out anywhere: someone tells you. Rosa mentions her sister. Carmen complains about the noise. The coach says the market's cousin is expecting you. If you forget, you ask — and the coach, who is the one person allowed to be a user interface, remembers on your behalf.

How do districts unlock? Pokémon Silver had gym badges; Pueblo has reputation. Handle enough of life in La Plaza — not a quiz, actual life: the missions, the small talk, being understood — and word spreads. Someone mentions you to their cousin in El Mercado. The cousin has a problem. Suddenly you have a reason to be there, and a person expecting you. The gate was never a wall; it was whether anyone over there knew your name.

4. The people are alive

The single most important design decision: NPCs are not chatbots with skins. They're residents. Each one is built from three parts:

The memory is where the game stops being software and starts being a place. Rosa remembers that you mangled quisiera on Tuesday, and on Thursday, when you nail it, she notices — out loud, with the exact teasing warmth of a person who watched you struggle. Doña Carmen remembers the flowerpot forever. You will earn forgiveness in installments, like in real life.

And because residents have schedules and relationships with each other, the town has weather systems of gossip. Help Tomás at the market and Rosa hears about it before you're back in the plaza. Your reputation isn't a number in a corner of the screen — it's distributed across the memories of everyone you've met, which is what a reputation actually is.

They speak at your level — and climb with you

Here's the trick that makes the whole thing work as a learning game. Every resident calibrates their Spanish to sit one small step above yours — the i+1 sweet spot. On day one, Rosa speaks to you the way you'd speak to a lost tourist: slow, simple, generous with gestures. Three weeks in, she's using the past tense at you, because you've earned it. The town gets harder exactly as fast as you do, which is a thing no textbook and no human immersion experience can actually promise.

4Real immersion is i+1 by luck: some strangers slow down for you, most don't. Pueblo is i+1 by design — every resident is running a model of what you can currently understand, and aims just past it. It's the one place the simulation is genuinely better than moving to Spain.

5. You have to actually talk

Passport is voice-first. You speak into your microphone; residents answer in actual audio, with actual voices — Rosa sounds like Rosa. Subtitles exist, in Spanish, as training wheels you'll eventually turn off. There is deliberately no "type instead" button on the main path.

5Accessibility note, because someone will rightly ask: a text mode will exist as an accessibility setting for players who can't use voice. It's the accommodation, not the default — the difference matters for everyone who can speak but would rather not, which is, notoriously, all of us.

Why be strict about this? Because speaking is the part everyone skips, and it's the part that is the skill:

  • Retrieval under pressure. Recognizing quisiera on a card is easy. Producing it while a baker looks at you expectantly is the actual sport. That difficulty is the workout.
  • Your mouth needs reps. Spanish requires sounds and rhythms English doesn't. You can't learn them silently, the same way you can't learn to whistle by reading.
  • Speaking anxiety is trained away by speaking. The number-one blocker for real-world learners isn't vocabulary, it's fear. A town of infinitely patient, never-mocking residents is the safest possible place to burn that fear off — hundreds of low-stakes reps before your first real Spanish conversation.

Panadería, day 4 · 07:58

ROSA: ¡Buenos días! ¿Lo de siempre?

Good morning! The usual?

YOU: Eh... sí. Dos... croissants. Y... quisiera un café.

ROSA: ¡Quisiera! Muy bien. El martes era "kee-see-EH-ra."

"Quisiera"! Very good. On Tuesday it was "kee-see-EH-ra."

YOU: ...gracias, Rosa.

6. Your coach

One person in Pueblo speaks English: your coach. Think of them as the friend who's lived there for years — the one who translates the town for you, not the language.

The coach is not a walking grammar textbook, and crucially, they're not in the conversation with you. Rosa never breaks character to explain the subjunctive; that would puncture the town. Instead, the coach catches you after:

  • Debriefs. "You did great with Tomás. You dodged the past tense twice, though — you said 'today' about a thing that happened yesterday. He noticed. Want to run it back?"
  • Pattern-spotting. The coach tracks what you're avoiding — the words you route around, the people you haven't dared talk to — and nudges you at it. This is what human tutors do that apps don't.
  • Pointing at doors. "Rosa's sister runs a stall in El Mercado. You've got the Spanish for it. Go say I sent you."

Progression, graded by life: when you finish a mission, there's no quiz. The LLM judges the thing that matters — did Rosa's sister actually get the message? Communication is the score. Grammar is just how you raise it.

7. How it actually works

The honest tech section. Every piece here exists and ships today — though some of it is still labelled "preview," which is a word you should read as "will move under you."

Why Rosa hears audio and not a transcript

The obvious way to build this is the cheap classic: speech-to-text, feed the text to an LLM, speak the answer back. Don't. Speech recognition is actively hostile to a game about pronunciation, because it's doing the opposite of your job — it has a language model inside it whose entire purpose is to guess what you meant. You mangle quisiera, and the transcript helpfully says "quisiera." The evidence is gone before Rosa ever sees it, and the one thing you needed the game to notice has been quietly cleaned up.

So we skip transcription entirely. The player's raw audio goes straight into a model that accepts audio natively, and Rosa reacts to how you actually said it. The remarkable part is the price: sending real audio instead of text adds well under a cent per hour of play. The most pedagogically important decision in the project is also nearly free.

The Flash-Lite trap Two things worth knowing if you're building this. First, no Flash-Lite model supports the realtime Live API — that's a different, pricier model tier. Second, and more surprising: the newest Flash-Lite is not the cheapest one. They've been getting more expensive with each release. What makes 3.5-flash-lite the right pick anyway is a quiet detail in the pricing table — it's the only one that bills audio input at the plain text rate instead of surcharging it. For a game that is nothing but audio input, that's the whole decision.
6Verified against Google's docs in August 2026. All the TTS and Live models here are still preview, so IDs and prices can move without deprecation notice — re-check before writing a cheque. Rough figures used above: audio in at $0.30/M tokens (~32 tokens/second of speech), streaming TTS at $20/M audio out.

What we're giving up, honestly

The premium option is Google's Live API — true native audio in and out, sub-second, the closest thing to a phone call. It runs roughly $0.30/hour versus our $0.05–0.15, which isn't the problem. The problem is that it's still a preview product, and we'd be betting a whole game's feel on it.

What the cheaper path costs us is real and worth naming: about a second of latency, and no interruption. You can't talk over Rosa. Real conversations are full of barge-in — that's a genuine loss, and if Phase 1 playtests say the town feels like a walkie-talkie, the Live API is the escape hatch and the architecture is designed so we can swap to it without touching anything else.

Judging speech fairly. One rule, and it's load-bearing: the game rewards communicating, never accent. The judge asks "did Rosa understand you?" — not "was that native?" A1 you gets credit for being understood at all; B2 you is held to a higher bar. Same as the real town, where nobody has ever been refused bread for rolling an R badly.

8. The hard part is not the AI. It's the town.

Everything up to here has been the fun part to design and, it turns out, the cheap part to build. Rosa's mind costs pennies an hour. Rosa's bakery — the tiles, the counter, the doorway that knows it's a doorway, the interior nobody sees until they walk in — is where projects like this actually die.

And the campaign structure makes it worse, because it multiplies. Four countries at around forty residents each is 160 characters who need sprite sheets, personas, schedules and mission hooks. Every shop that gives you a mission needs an interior, which means there are more interior maps than there is overworld. This is the part where the honest thing to do is admit that a person cannot draw their way out of it.

So the design rule for the entire world layer is: don't draw anything you can inherit, and don't build anything someone has already given away.

What we're standing on

The character decision deserves a specific mention, because it's where 3D quietly beats pixel art at our own game. The pixel-art plan needed 160 hand-assembled sprite sheets; the 3D plan needs one rigged character, a wardrobe of swappable meshes, and a palette per country. Every resident shares a skeleton and one set of animations — Rosa and a Tokyo shopkeeper differ by clothes, colours and animation timing, not by artwork. And a 3D face can do the thing this game actually runs on: look confused, patient, or delighted while you fumble a sentence.

Why not Unity

The reasonable question. The answer is that this game's core input is a live microphone stream, and the big engines treat that as an edge case in web builds: Unity's Microphone class isn't supported in WebGL at all — you bolt on a community JavaScript shim — and Godot's own microphone docs list Windows, macOS, Linux, Android and iOS, with web conspicuously absent. The mechanic the entire game rests on would be the one thing fighting the engine.

In JavaScript it's the paved road instead: microphone into an audio worklet into a socket, which is exactly the shape the model wants. And browser-first buys what Phase 1 needs most — you send someone a link, they allow the mic, and thirty seconds later they're talking to Rosa. No install, no per-platform build. When the question under test is "is this magical," friction is the enemy.

It helps that rendering isn't where the risk lives. Drawing a 2D tile town is solved everywhere; voice latency and NPC memory are not. Nor is "better graphics" really an engine question — Sea of Stars is a 2D game, and what makes it beautiful is art direction, not the renderer. The ceiling here is the quality of the tiles and sprites.

The hedge is that almost none of this is engine-locked. The town generator emits data, glTF models load anywhere, and the cast, missions and learner model are engine-agnostic — so if the browser turns out to be the wrong bet, only the renderer is thrown away. If it ever does go native, the pick is Godot — free, MIT, and comfortable in 3D — not Unity.

The town generator we do have to write

Here's where the research disappointed, usefully. There are lovely procedural city generators out there, and none of them can generate this game's town — because in Pueblo a building is not scenery. Every building is a place with a purpose and a person in it: this one is a bakery, the bakery has Rosa, Rosa gives you the bread mission. Generic generators model streets and rooftops. They have no concept of the thing that matters.

Wave Function Collapse deserves its own warning here, because it's the algorithm everyone reaches for and it's the wrong tool. WFC gives you beautiful texture and incoherent structure — it will happily produce a town whose streets don't reach the plaza. Developers who've shipped it publicly concluded it doesn't survive contact with a real game. Use it for grass and clutter; never for the map.

7The other trap is licensing, and it bites late. Most of the good free art is CC-BY-SA, which is copyleft: art derivatives — including AI reskins — have to be shared alike, and every asset needs crediting. That's survivable, but only if the credits file is generated from day one. Reconstructing the provenance of three hundred assets at launch is a genuinely miserable week.

So we write one modest generator ourselves: plaza → main street → side streets → lots → buildings tagged by function, emitted as Tiled JSON, then hand-polished. The renderer reads that logical map and instantiates the 3D scene — building meshes on the footprints, props on the furniture markers — which is how the same town data would survive even an engine swap. It's a few hundred lines, and it's the piece that makes a country a config file.

Where AI actually helps with art (and where it doesn't)

Worth being precise, because the intuition is backwards. Image models in 2026 are genuinely good at concept art, portraits and textures, and still bad at game-ready geometry — generated meshes arrive with broken topology, no rig, and no consistency across a family of assets. The reliable wins are upstream and around the edges: concept sheets to hand a modeller (or to guide kit-bashing), palette and material exploration, and texture variation on models whose geometry is already right.

So the useful move isn't "generate a Japanese tileset." It's reskinning geometry that's already correct: take the base tiles, whose grid and perspective are known-good, and restyle the facades and palette per country. Spain's warm plaster and terracotta; Japan's tile roofs and signage. The hard structural work is inherited; the model only changes how it looks.

And the bigger lever isn't pixels at all. The thing an LLM should be mass-producing for this project is content — the forty personas, their schedules, their mission hooks, their opinions about each other. That's the bottleneck that actually scales with campaigns, and it's the one we're best equipped to automate.

The rule that decides whether campaign #2 ever exists Every world asset must be produced by a script, not a session. If making a resident means opening an art tool, then 160 residents means the project quietly ends after Spain. The pipeline has to be automated during campaign #1, when it feels like premature optimisation — because that's the only moment it's still cheap to do.

9. The roadmap

The build order is ruthless about one thing: the magic moment — you say something in Spanish out loud, and a character who remembers you answers — must exist in Phase 1, or nothing else matters. Everything after it is scaffolding around a thing we already know works.

One last thing. The saddest object in language learning is the streak — a number that measures the only thing that was never the point: showing up to an app. Passport doesn't have a streak. It has Rosa wondering where you've been, and telling you so, in Spanish, when you finally come back.

That's the retention mechanic. It's called a life.
